Making the Elite: Caste, Class, and Cultural Capital in Multinational Hiring Revising

NYU Political Economy, University of Chicago, NBER Race and Stratification, MIT Organizational Economics
Short Summary: Twitter thread
Coverage: Steven Pinker (x2); Raghuram Rajan; Alice Evans; Marginal Revolution
Where do class disparities emerge in elite hiring, and why? I study over 1,000 multinational jobs recruiting at an elite Indian college, using state-codified caste categories as a proxy for cumulative social disadvantage. Almost the entire caste penalty in success rates across screening rounds emerges at final-round personal interviews that assess “fit.” Disadvantaged-caste candidates are nearly 30 percentage points less likely to convert these interviews into offers; caste gaps in advancement through earlier screening rounds—application screening, aptitude tests, and group debates—are close to zero. Two tests help assess whether personal interviews make caste legible through class-coded biographical cues. First, nearly one million words of public mock transcripts show how the preparation ecosystem teaches candidates to prepare for elite jobs. Mock personal interviews are more likely to elicit information about hometown, schooling, hobbies, family background, and parents’ occupation, whereas other oral screening rounds, such as group debates, center on debate topics rather than class-coded biography. Second, the timing of penalties differs by surname identifiability: penalties immediately emerge at first-round application screening for the 15% with caste-identifiable surnames, but only after final-round personal interviews for the 85% with caste-ambiguous surnames. Together, these tests suggest that most of the caste penalty is linked to caste becoming legible through biography, consistent with recruiters reading these class-coded cues as signs of cultural capital and elite belonging. Post-hire outcomes are more consistent with bias than with simple statistical or customer discrimination: disadvantaged-caste hires are promoted more and leave their first employers less often within three years, across role types and job-selectivity tiers. Interviews with senior HR heads at multinationals suggest several reasons biased fit screening can persist. Screeners are typically distinct from eventual supervisors, and recruiting teams are judged on process metrics, such as hiring turnaround time and offer drop rates, rather than the subsequent performance of those they hire.

Easy Interviews with Jason Sockin and Elliott Ash Submitted

NBER Summer Institute (Labor and Personnel), NBER Organizational Economics, CEPR IMO-ESF
Short Summary: Twitter thread
Links: CEPR Working Paper
Interviews allow employers to learn about workers, but do they also enable workers to learn about firms? Studying 500,000 interview reports from Glassdoor, we find candidates for high-paying jobs are more likely to reject a job offer if they believe the interview was easy. Easy interviews appear to convey poor "fit" as those who accept offers after easy interviews are two-fifths of a standard deviation less satisfied with their jobs and 10 percent less likely to remain with their employer for at least one year. Analysis of interview narratives using large language models reveals difficult interviews signal colleague ability whereas easy interviews convey a nonselective process. In a small-scale randomized field experiment, an exogenous increase in difficulty elevated perceived difficulty and boosted applicant engagement with the vacancy. Interviews offer workers a preview of match quality, highlighting a channel through which labor markets may become less efficient if firms automate hiring with AI.

Pyramids, Diamonds, and Oscillations: AI and the Structure of Internal Labor Markets with Guido Friebel, Yao Huang, Jin Li, and Andrew Zhang

NBER Organizational Economics, Cologne Summer School, CEPR IMO-ESF
Links: NBER Conference Paper; SSRN
Coverage: Garry Tan (CEO of Y Combinator); Kyla Scanlon; Business Insider
AI is changing workplaces: it leads to higher individual productivity and faster learning. We model the effects of these changes on internal labor markets. We show that for productivity shocks, firms preserve the span in the long run: a pyramid remains a pyramid. In the short run, however, firms freeze junior hiring, temporarily transforming the pyramid toward a diamond. In the transition to the new steady state, firms may oscillate between pyramids and diamonds before settling. For learning shocks, the long-run span falls, potentially permanently shifting the firm from a pyramid to a diamond. These fluctuations create inequality between junior cohorts and affect firm value when human capital is firm-specific.
